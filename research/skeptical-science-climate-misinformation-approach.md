## Findings

### 1. Mission and framing

Skeptical Science states its mission as: "debunk misinformation that is harming our species' ability to deal with climate change caused by excessive anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. We do this by presenting peer-reviewed science and explaining the techniques of climate science denial, discourses of climate delay, and climate solutions denial." [S4]

The organization describes itself as a non-profit 501(c)(3) science-education organization, run by a global volunteer team, with all original material passing an internal peer-review process and an editorial policy; content is translated into 20+ languages and the site is licensed CC BY 3.0 [S4]. It won the 2011 Australian Museum Eureka Prize for Advancement of Climate Change Knowledge and the 2016 NCSE "Friend of the Planet" award [S5]. It was founded in 2007 by John Cook after reading a 2007 speech by Senator James Inhofe calling global warming a hoax [S11 — secondary].

### 2. Core philosophy: skepticism vs. denial

The site's foundational distinction is: **"Skepticism is a process, denial is a position."** Genuine skepticism means "the open-minded consideration of something based on the evidence… you look at all the facts before coming to a conclusion," whereas denialism "looks at small pieces of the puzzle while neglecting the full picture," attacking evidence that contradicts a preferred answer while uncritically embracing anything that seems to refute the consensus. "If 99 pieces of evidence support an idea and 1 doesn't, a skeptic says 'that idea is probably true'. A denier says 'Ahah, that idea is false'." [S5, S6]

The 2010 Guide puts the same idea as: "when you take a close look at arguments expressing climate 'skepticism', what you often observe is cherry picking of pieces of evidence while rejecting any data that doesn't fit the desired picture. This isn't skepticism. It is ignoring facts and the science." [S3]

### 3. The three-step approach: myth → technique → full picture

The stated approach: "It looks at the many climate myths, exposes the techniques and fallacies used to distort the science and then puts them in their proper context by presenting the full picture." [S5; identical logic in S6]

Concretely, that means each myth gets (a) a plain statement of what the science says, (b) an exposure of the rhetorical/logical technique the myth uses, and (c) the full body of evidence in context — not a single counter-study. The Guide illustrates this with the "human CO2 is tiny compared to natural emissions" myth: the missing half of the picture is that nature also absorbs CO2, and human emissions upset the balance, leaving about half of our emissions in the air [S3]. It catalogs **10 "human fingerprints" on climate change** (less heat escaping to space, cooling upper atmosphere, nights warming faster than days, more fossil-fuel carbon in air and coral, etc.) and stresses the case "isn't based on a show of hands but on direct observations — multiple, independent lines of evidence all point to the same answer," including the >97% consensus among actively publishing climate scientists [S3].

### 4. The encyclopedic rebuttal database (the core product)

- The argument list [S1] is browsable **by taxonomy, by popularity** (how often each argument appears in online articles), with **fixed numbers** for permanent references, short URLs, and a print-friendly view [S1, S5]. Top-level myth categories shown on the site include "Climate's changed before," "It's the sun," "It's not bad," "There is no consensus," "It's cooling," "Models are unreliable," "Temp record is unreliable," "It hasn't warmed since 1998," "Antarctica is gaining ice."
- Each argument has a **one-line rebuttal** (e.g., "Sun & climate: moving in opposite directions — in the last 35 years of global warming, the sun has shown a slight cooling trend" [S8]) and a dedicated **one-liners page** [S9], plus a detailed argument page with peer-reviewed references (the Guide carries ~60 numbered references [S3]).
- Beyond the list: "Misinformation by Source," "Politician Climate Myths," "Links by argument," a "Trend Calculator," climate graphics (the "Escalator," free to republish), a glossary, and mobile apps [S1 navigation].

### 5. The debunking formula: Fact – Myth – Fallacy

Cook's posted guidance: "An effective rebuttal requires three elements. **Fact. Myth. Fallacy.**" Specifically: (1) provide a replacement explanation that fits the causal links the myth left in people's mental model — "the golden rule of debunking is fight sticky myths with stickier facts"; (2) do mention the myth so it can be tagged as false in the mental model, but **warn people before mentioning it** so they are cognitively on guard; (3) explain the fallacy or rhetorical technique the misinformation uses, resolving the fact-vs-myth conflict [S7].

### 6. The Scientific Guide to Global Warming Skepticism (S2, S3)

The flagship publication (John Cook, first published December 2010, CC BY-NC 3.0, acknowledged by ~20 climate scientists) "looks at both the evidence that human activity is causing global warming and the ways that climate 'skeptic' arguments can mislead by presenting only small pieces of the puzzle rather than the full picture" [S3]. Structure: what skepticism really is → human fingerprints → evidence warming is happening → past climate change → climate sensitivity → impacts → "shooting the messenger" (e.g., Climategate: six independent inquiries cleared the scientists; out-of-context quotes deflect from the science) → the consensus of evidence (>97%) [S3]. Note: the HTML landing page itself warns the guide (Dec 2010) "is no longer completely current" and points readers to newer linked material [S2].

### 7. Ecosystem and research backing (beyond the website)

Per SkS's own EGU24 abstract [S10], team members "actively research best-practices refutation techniques," producing resources that apply across topics: the **FLICC taxonomy** of science-denial techniques (**F**ake experts, **L**ogical fallacies, **I**mpossible expectations, **C**herry picking, **C**onspiracy theories), the **Debunking Handbook 2020**, and the **Conspiracy Theory Handbook**. Cook also led the 2013 ERL paper quantifying the 97% consensus (widely cited, "Best article of 2013" per ERL's editorial board) [S11 — secondary], developed the Denial101x MOOC with University of Queensland (40,000+ enrollments) and the Cranky Uncle game [S10, S11 — secondary]. A 2025 preprint notes SkS's ~193 rebuttals and its myth taxonomy served as the starting point for downstream misinformation research [egusphere-2025-3812, seen via search snippet — secondary].

## Takeaways for building a misinformation-combating skill

1. **Enumerate the myths first** in a browsable taxonomy (plus popularity ranking and stable IDs) — the database of arguments is the product; corrections are indexed to myths, not to events [S1, S5].
2. **Three-part structure per myth:** one-line rebuttal → detailed evidence-based explanation (multiple depth levels, peer-reviewed citations) → the fallacy/technique the myth uses (FLICC-style) [S1, S3, S7, S8, S10].
3. **Correct with Fact–Myth–Fallacy:** warn before naming the myth, give a sticky replacement fact that fills the causal gap, then explain the distortion technique [S7].
4. **Always present the full picture / multiple independent lines of evidence** rather than a single counter-study; frame consensus as "consensus of evidence," not authority [S3, S5].
5. **Separate skepticism (process) from denial (position)** in the framing language — it is the site's core rhetorical move [S5, S6].
6. **Make content reusable and licensed**: CC-licensed, translated, print/short-URL/app formats, graphics free to republish [S1, S4, S5].
7. **Institutional trust signals**: volunteer-run non-profit, internal peer review, editorial policy, corrections channel, awards/endorsements [S4, S5].
